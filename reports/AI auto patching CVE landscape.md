# Build the patch-policy brain, skip the autopatcher

Short answer: don't build "AI reads security news and patches my app by itself." Build the narrower piece that is still missing: a **policy layer that decides whether and how fast to patch**. It sits on top of tools that already do the patching (Renovate, Dependabot, osv-scanner), opens evidence-backed PRs, and leaves the merge to a human. Every piece of the loop you described already exists somewhere. GitHub hands Dependabot alerts to Claude, Codex or Copilot agents ([GitHub](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-04-07-dependabot-alerts-are-now-assignable-to-ai-agents-for-remediation)). Snyk, Aikido, Google, OpenAI and Anthropic all ship find-and-fix agents. Google's Mantis and Anthropic's reference harness are open source. **Nobody publicly ships the whole loop, and nobody uses security news as a trigger.** Big companies auto-merge deterministic dependency bumps at huge scale. None of them is documented auto-merging AI-written fixes for new CVEs, and they have good reason: when benchmarks check fixes properly, the best AI patchers really fix only about half of the bugs they appear to fix ([PatchBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04075)). The "artex" you saw is almost certainly ARTEX, a Chinese AI *offensive* pentest framework that has been tied to real bank intrusions. It doesn't patch anything. Don't use it. On models, flip your plan around. A deterministic pipeline is the orchestrator. Cheap Chinese models only read public advisory and news text. Claude writes the code. Tests, re-scans and a human decide what ships. Use Hermes only as an optional notifier, and never connect your Claude subscription login to it. Most of the research held up under four independent fact-checks, and the corrections are listed at the end.

## Verdict at a glance

| Goal | Verdict | Why | What to actually build |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fun open-source project** | **Worth building, if you keep the scope tight** | The gap is real and documented, the topics are frontier (agent sandboxing, prompt-injection-safe design, model routing, verification), and "agentic security" repos draw large audiences | A cooldown-vs-urgency policy engine plus evidence-carrying PRs, for one ecosystem, with a published eval table and a gallery of failures |
| **Tool for your own apps** | **A thin version (about 2 weekends) is worth it. The full vision is a waste** | Renovate, Dependabot and osv-scanner already cover most of the value for a solo dev (a rough judgment, not a measurement). You'll see maybe a few relevant CVEs a month | Turn on Dependabot alerts and Renovate with cooldown and grouping first. Then add a small policy step and a bounded AI repair step for broken builds |
| **Startup** | **Waste of time as a standalone product** | GitHub, Snyk, Aikido, Semgrep and the frontier labs are all shipping this in 2026, and 2026 seed rounds are already chasing "autonomous remediation." For Western enterprise buyers, "cheap Chinese models" counts against you in procurement | Revisit only if the OSS version gets real adoption and inbound requests. GitHub is both the natural acquirer and the natural killer |

The headline should not be "reads news, patches and merges on its own." It should be "explains why this CVE matters to *your* repo, then opens a verified PR with the evidence attached."

## "Artex" is an offensive pentest kit, not a patcher

What you saw is almost certainly **ARTEX**. GitHub user Autumn-27 (who also wrote ScopeSentry) built it as an LLM multi-agent *autonomous penetration-testing* platform: a Go backend with an embedded Next.js console and PostgreSQL, published around July 26, 2026 under AGPL-3.0 ([BaaRai/ARTEX README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/BaaRai/ARTEX/HEAD/README.md); [SOCRadar](https://socradar.io/blog/artex-ai-pentest-tool-what-security-teams-need-to-know/)). Its agent roles are **goals, planner, worker, mainagent** (human-in-the-loop), and later builds add **retester**. You may have seen "conductor/worker/report agents" elsewhere, which is wrong. The closest ARTEX gets to patching is that retester loop: it re-runs a finding against a fixed version and records "still reproducible / fixed / cannot confirm." It **verifies fixes but never writes them** ([BaaRai/ARTEX](https://github.com/BaaRai/ARTEX)).

The upstream **Autumn-27/ARTEX is gone**. Its last commit (v0.3.15) is dated October 7, 2026, and a fork maintainer says the author deleted the repo. The developer cited misuse and said the project would go closed-source ([jaykwok/ARTEX](https://github.com/jaykwok/ARTEX); [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/developer-of-hacked-bank-tool-artex-pulls-it-from-public-github/)). The largest live copy is **BaaRai/ARTEX** (about 9.8k stars), followed by jiwoochris/artex-global, RuoJi6/ARTEX and Hinln/ARTEX. The trouble around it is serious. CrowdStrike and Recorded Future tied ARTEX to intrusions at South Korean financial firms in late September and early October 2026 ([Recorded Future](https://www.recordedfuture.com/blog/tag-217-artex-south-korean-banks)). In those intrusions, one attacker console used DeepSeek as its primary model, with GLM and Grok as backups ([Hunt.io](https://hunt.io/blog/artex-south-korea-bank-infrastructure)). The FBI issued a FLASH alert on November 13, 2026 reporting more than 1,600 exposed consoles and some intrusions that reached privileged access in under three hours ([Industrial Cyber](https://industrialcyber.co/industrial-cyber-attacks/fbi-warns-hackers-weaponizing-artex-ai-framework-to-automate-attacks-on-energy-manufacturing-telecom/)). Separately, backdoored Docker images (tomicdd/artex and congdonglinux/artex) shipped an unauthenticated root shell until Docker removed them on November 5 ([Kodem](https://www.kodemsecurity.com/resources/artex-ai-pentest-framework-backdoored-docker-image-iocs-response-runbook)). Google's threat-intel group also names ARTEX among the agentic attack frameworks it tracks ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/google-finds-hackers-using-ai-agents-to.html)).

**Verdict: don't integrate ARTEX or any fork, and keep your project's branding away from it.** If you meant something else, two names come close. Stanford's **ARTEMIS** is the ICLR 2026 pentest agent that beat 9 of 10 human testers ([ICLR](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/0410c2ff9f872efe5a7c61a4323a5da3-Abstract-Conference.html); [repo](https://github.com/Stanford-Trinity/ARTEMIS)). **IlyassCODEX/ARTEX** is an unrelated recon tool from 2025. Neither one patches code.

## Every piece exists, but nobody ships the full loop

Each independent research track came to the same conclusion. No product or open-source project is **publicly documented** doing all five steps: watch CVE feeds *and* security news, decide whether your repo is affected, patch, test, and open a PR. The market checker agreed, with one caveat: vendor marketing from Ona and Seal *implies* "CVE published, then patch and PR," and that couldn't be verified. Treat the gap as **real but thinner than it looks**.

### The Chinese ecosystem: lots of watching and attacking, little repairing

China's open-source scene is strongest at **intel push bots** and **offensive agents**. [zema1/watchvuln](https://github.com/zema1/watchvuln) (1.9k★) is the reference design. It polls Chinese sources (Alibaba AVD, QiAnXin TI, OSCS, ThreatBook, Seebug, Chaitin, Venustech), de-duplicates by CVE, and pushes alerts to DingTalk, WeCom, Feishu or Slack. Its forks add KEV and a "copy analysis prompt to DeepSeek" button, but none of them remediate. The most-starred Chinese AI security repos are audit and attack tools, such as [lintsinghua/DeepAudit](https://github.com/lintsinghua/DeepAudit) (7.3k★). DeepAudit's roadmap lists "agent opens a fix PR" as **not yet done**. Vendors are fighting a benchmark war on CyberGym, which measures vulnerability *reproduction*, not patching. Tencent Xuanwu reports 84% with GLM-5.1 ([Tencent XLab](https://xlab.tencent.com/en/2026/07/02/xuanwu-atuin-cybergym/)). Sangfor reports 86.3% ([Sangfor](https://www.sangfor.com/es/news-and-press-release/sangfor-ai-ranked-4-on-cybergym-2026)). 360 claims 99.4%, which can't be verified ([Remio](https://www.remio.ai/post/360-cybergym-results-tulongfeng-claims-99-4-vulnerability-reproduction)). All of these are self-reported. Chinese production auto-remediation shows up only in narrow, verifiable areas: ByteDance's vArmor turns a CVE into AppArmor/Seccomp mitigation policy rather than a code patch ([vArmor](https://github.com/bytedance/vArmor/blob/main/website/blog/2026-05-08-varmor-vuln-policy-generator/index.md)), and OpenAnolis generates kernel hot patches. The most reusable Chinese asset for you is **ByteDance's [PatchEval](https://github.com/bytedance/PatchEval)**, a patching benchmark of 1,000 CVEs across Go, JS and Python. Interesting detail: under Tencent's own scaffold, GLM-5.1 scored 84% on CyberGym versus 68.7% for the same model under Claude Code. The harness matters about as much as the model.

### The feed landscape changed under everyone in 2026

Don't build on NVD. On April 15, 2026, NIST began enriching only KEV, federal-software and "critical software" CVEs. Roughly 29,000 older backlogged CVEs moved to "Not Scheduled" ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/1910421); [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/16/nist-national-vulnerability-database-nvd-enrichment/)). Without CPE data, CPE-matching tools can't see most new CVEs. Match packages with OSV/GHSA (OSV's querybatch takes up to 1,000 package versions per call, per its [Swagger spec](https://osv.dev/docs/osv_service_v1.swagger.json)), prioritize with CISA KEV and FIRST EPSS, or take cve.now's merged feed instead of scraping sources yourself.

## Benchmarks say half of "fixed" isn't really fixed

This is the core technical risk, and the numbers are sobering.

The DARPA AIxCC final (August 2025) found 54 of 63 synthetic vulnerabilities (86%) and patched 43 (68%). Teams also found 18 real bugs and submitted 11 real patches. The average cost was about **$152 per successful task**, and a task could be a proof of vulnerability, a patch, or a bundle ([DARPA](https://darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results)). Atlantis won with 392.76 points and 91.27% submission accuracy ([Trail of Bits deck](https://www.trailofbits.com/documents/AIxCC_Main_Stage_Presentation.pdf)). Then an independent re-evaluation, **PatchBench** (September 2026), reported that **the top three AIxCC systems pass more than 97% of the original PoCs but solve only about half the tasks** once security and semantic validation are added. Across 11 agents, PoC-only validation **inflates the solve rate by 1.83x** ([PatchBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04075)). Even within AIxCC, patches submitted without a proof of vulnerability were unreliable ([SoK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07666)). That's your exact situation, because most CVEs don't come with a PoC that runs against *your* app.

| Benchmark | Headline | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **AutoPatchBench** (Meta, 2025) | Gemini 1.5 Pro **generated** patches 61.1% of the time, but only **5.3%** of the total set was correct after fuzzing and differential testing ([Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2025/04/29/ai-research/autopatchbench-benchmark-ai-powered-security-fixes/)) | A patch that looks plausible is very different from a correct one |
| **SEC-bench** (NeurIPS 2025) | At most **34.0%** patching and 18.0% PoC generation across agents ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11791)) | Mid-2025 repo-level baseline |
| **PatchEval** (2025) | The original paper's best system fixed 23.0% (53/230). **That number is stale.** PatchEval-Verified (July 2026) lists 83.9% for a Codex setup, using a **changed harness**, so the two numbers can't be compared ([repo](https://github.com/bytedance/PatchEval)) | How the test oracle is designed dominates the measured rate |
| **Byam / BUMP** (2025) | o3-mini got **27% build success** repairing breaking Java dependency updates; DeepSeek V3 got 21% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07522v3)). An FSE'25 agentic approach reached up to 23% ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3729366)) | Repairing breaking changes, which is the actually useful case, is weak |
| **Vul4J** (2026) | 24.8% of 319 patches were fully correct, but that's **one model, zero-shot** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10072)) | Not a general ceiling |
| **BountyBench** (2025) | Codex CLI and Claude Code patched 87.5–90% of known, localized web bugs, with the exploit as the oracle ([NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/faed4276b52ef762879db4142655c699-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)) | Easy when you have an exploit oracle |
| **Silent failures** (2026) | 170 patches that **passed tests but kept or introduced vulnerabilities** ([arXiv 2609.10548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.10548)) | "CI is green" doesn't prove the fix |
| **Multi-language study** (2026) | 7–24% over-fixing, meaning functionality got removed. **LLM-as-judge is unreliable** for checking that behavior is preserved ([JCP](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcp6050153)) | Don't let a model grade a model |

What this means for you: the safe zone is fixes that have a strong *executable* oracle. A version bump to the first fixed version, plus your tests, plus a re-scan showing the advisory is gone, is about as strong as it gets. A backport validated by the upstream fix commit's own regression test is next. Free-form code fixes without a PoC are where AI patching quietly fails. Claims above 80% in papers almost always rely on the weak "PoC no longer crashes" check.

## Practitioners want fewer, smarter PRs, not more autonomy

Community sentiment was harder to gather directly, because Reddit, Hacker News and X couldn't be fetched (see the verification section). The press and blog record is clear, though. The reference post of 2026 is Filippo Valsorda's **"Turn Dependabot Off."** Dependabot flagged thousands of repos that never called the vulnerable function, which trains people to ignore alerts. He suggests running govulncheck on a schedule and doing impact assessment by hand instead ([Valsorda](https://words.filippo.io/dependabot/); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/24/github_dependabot_noise_machine/)). Backlog data backs this up. Bot PRs for NuGet and Gradle take 6–7 business days to merge, and more than half of the open ones have failing builds, because "bots can't fix breaking changes" ([nesbitt.io](https://nesbitt.io/2026/01/10/16-best-practices-for-reducing-dependabot-noise.html)). An HN thread on reducing Dependabot noise (seen only through a search summary) leaned toward auto-merging after green checks, with policy controls ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46583914)).

Maintainers see AI on the receiving end. curl ended its HackerOne bounty in January 2026 after a flood of AI-generated slop reports ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/21/curl_ends_bug_bounty/)). Yet Daniel Stenberg praised roughly 50 real fixes that came from AI tools when a skilled human did the triage ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/2/curl/)). The Linux kernel published 432 CVEs in two days in July 2026. That was partly a backlog, not purely AI ([The Register](https://theregister.com/security/2026/07/22/linux-kernel-team-publishes-432-cves-in-two-days/5276497)). One useful data point for you: a triage of 481 kernel CVEs found that none needed an emergency patch, and it cost about **$192 in LLM tokens** ([oss-security](https://seclists.org/oss-sec/2026/q3/230)). Cheap model triage of public advisory text works.

The other big theme is a tension nobody has tooling for. After the Shai-Hulud npm worm waves, **release cooldowns became mainstream advice**: Elastic moved to 14 days, and Zscaler recommends cooldowns ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/shai-hulud-worm-2-0-updated-response); [Zscaler](https://www.zscaler.com/jp/blogs/security-research/tracking-shai-hulud-inside-chaindrop-npm-worm)). One variant even earned a legitimate provenance attestation ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/the-shai-hulud-npm-worm-didnt-fake-its-security-check-it-earned-a-legitimate-one)). Meanwhile, Google's threat-intel group reported in November 2026 that attackers run an "exploit_agent" that diffs patches and drafts exploits with no human involved. Google gave no measured patch-to-exploit time, and the agents hallucinated ([CSO](https://www.csoonline.com/article/4249595/google-spots-the-first-hacker-ai-system-that-finds-and-exploits-flaws-autonomously.html)). So the advice is "wait before updating" *and* "patch faster." That collision is your opening.

## Three gaps are genuinely missing

**1. A cooldown-vs-urgency policy engine (the best bet).** Nobody resolves that conflict update by update. The rule you'd encode: hold routine bumps for N days, and fast-track when the CVE is in KEV, has a high EPSS score, or has credible exploit news **and** your code actually reaches the vulnerable part. Even then, fast-track only after provenance, maintainer-change and install-script checks on the *new* version. A fast-track bypasses your cooldown, and that's exactly when a poisoned "fix" release hurts most. The engine is small, testable and explainable, and nobody has built it.

**2. Reachability-gated triage that explains itself.** Answer Valsorda directly: "here's why this PR exists," or "here's why I didn't open one." For Go, use govulncheck. For npm and Python, have an LLM *propose* the vulnerable functions from the fix commit and a static tool *check* whether your code calls them. Reachability for JS and Python is weak, so reachability should only ever **lower urgency. It must never silence a KEV item.**

**3. News as an early-warning signal (real, but the least proven).** For big-vendor zero-days, news often lands hours to days before structured data. For a typical app's dependency tree, though, GHSA, OSV and KEV usually catch up the same day. That claim is an inference; no study measured news-versus-GHSA lead time. Use news only to **raise priority on a watch list, never to trigger an action**, and measure it. "News flagged X hours before GHSA on N of M cases" is a publishable result either way.

Skip first-party vulnerability hunting (Mantis, Strix and Claude Security already own it), C/C++ fuzzing territory (Buttercup and OSS-CRS), and anything that merges to production on its own.

## Route models by data sensitivity, and let tests do the judging

Your instinct to use cheap models for bulk work and Claude for hard work is right. Two parts of the plan need changing. **A cheap model should never be the overseer of a strong one.** Executable checks are the gate. And **where your data goes matters more than which task a model does.**

| Job | Model | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance filter on news and advisories | Cheapest open-weight model (small Qwen or DeepSeek "flash" class) | High volume, public text, no tools, no secrets |
| Structured extraction from public text (vendor, product, versions, CVE IDs, exploited?, fixed?) | Mid-tier open-weight (DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi) | Public text only. Strict JSON schema with a verbatim evidence quote for every field, checked by a validator |
| Triage write-ups, VEX drafts, guesses at vulnerable functions | Open-weight on public data. **Claude whenever private repo context is involved** | Once private code is in the prompt, the data rules below apply |
| Bump repair and code edits | **Claude Code headless, on an API key** | This is where quality matters. Optionally test an open-weight model in the same slot inside an OpenHands or SWE-agent harness, measured with your eval set |
| Second-opinion review | A **different model family** from the one that wrote the patch | Reduces correlated failure. Advisory only: it can never override the executable gates |
| Choosing package names and versions | **No model.** Take versions from OSV's "fixed" ranges | Open models hallucinated package names 21.7% of the time vs 5.2% for commercial ones in a USENIX 2025 study of 2024-era models ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/spracklen)) |

**On Chinese models specifically.** Capability isn't the problem: DeepSeek V3 came close to o3-mini on breaking-change repair (21% vs 27%), and GLM backbones lead Chinese CyberGym entries. The problem is **data exposure**. Sending private source code, dependency lists or repo structure to hosted DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM or Kimi APIs puts that data under retention policies and a jurisdiction you can't audit. For public advisory text that's fine. For anything private, use the same open weights through a US host or on your own GPU, or keep that work on Claude. If you ever deal with *undisclosed* vulnerabilities or Chinese products, China's 2021 vulnerability regulation also comes into play. It requires product providers to report to MIIT within 2 days ([CAC](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2021-07/13/c_1627761607640342.htm)). Reposts say it also bans publishing before the vendor's fix and sharing undisclosed vulns with foreign parties, though the checker couldn't confirm that text against the primary source. Stay on public advisories and the regulation won't touch you.

**On your Claude subscription. This is the key correction.** Running `claude -p` or the Agent SDK while signed in with your plan **draws from your normal subscription limits**. Anthropic had announced a separate credit pool for this, then **paused** that plan on June 15, 2026. Since October 7, 2026, Max, Ultra and Team plans include monthly API credits, but those apply only when you run with an **API key** from the linked Console org ([Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/15036540-use-the-claude-agent-sdk-with-your-claude-plan)). Anthropic's legal page says subscription OAuth is for "ordinary use of Claude Code and other native Anthropic applications." It also says developers may not offer Claude.ai login or "route requests through Free, Pro, Max, or Ultra plan credentials on behalf of their users," and that **products serving others must use API keys** ([Claude Code legal](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/legal-and-compliance)). In practice: experiment with `claude -p` on your own repos under your plan limits. Run the unattended scheduled bot on an API key, ideally drawing on those included monthly API credits. Never ship an OSS tool that asks users for their subscription login.

**On Hermes Agent.** It's real and solid: NousResearch/hermes-agent is MIT-licensed, very actively maintained, model-agnostic (z.ai/GLM, Kimi, MiniMax, OpenRouter, Anthropic), and has a built-in cron scheduler that delivers to Telegram, Discord, Slack and other platforms, plus isolated subagents ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/HEAD/README.md); [providers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/HEAD/website/docs/integrations/providers.md)). But it's a *general personal agent* with persistent memory. A patch pipeline wants a boring, auditable, deterministic orchestrator: GitHub Actions cron or a systemd timer driving a Python pipeline. Hermes's docs also offer "Claude Max via OAuth" as a provider option. Under Anthropic's stated policy that looks non-compliant, though Anthropic hasn't ruled on Hermes specifically. So **use Hermes, if you want it at all, only as the chat notifier** ("approve PR #12?"). Give it no repo write access, and don't connect your Claude subscription login to it.

## Eight risks decide whether this is safe to run

| Risk | Evidence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Wrong patches** | Top AIxCC systems really fix only about half of what passes PoCs; 170 test-passing silent failures; LLM judges unreliable (sources above) | Restrict AI to cases with strong oracles: tests, re-scan, upstream regression tests. Humans merge anything with LLM-written code |
| **2. Prompt injection** | PromptPwnd: AI agents in GitHub Actions leaked secrets, affecting at least five Fortune 500 companies ([Aikido](https://aikido.dev/blog/promptpwnd-github-actions-ai-agents)). Clinejection: an issue title led to cache poisoning and a malicious npm publish ([Khan](https://adnanthekhan.com/posts/clinejection)). A Claude Code Action flaw was fixed in v2.1.128 ([DevOps.com](https://devops.com/security-flaw-in-claude-code-illustrates-the-risk-of-ai-in-developer-workflows)) | The extractor model gets no tools and no secrets, and its output must pass schema validation. The patcher never sees raw news. Changelogs go in delimited data blocks. Egress allowlist, short-lived scoped tokens, never `workflows:write` |
| **3. Malicious "fix" releases** | Shai-Hulud waves, including one with real provenance | Check provenance, maintainer changes, install scripts and package age before any fast-track. Pin by hash. Auto-merge only Tier A |
| **4. Terms of service** | Subscription OAuth limits (above) | API key for unattended runs. Never accept or route users' plan logins |
| **5. Data exposure to Chinese APIs** | PRC-hosted retention you can't audit | Public text only on hosted Chinese APIs. Private context goes to Claude, a US host, or self-hosted weights |
| **6. Regulation and embargoes** | China's 2021 vulnerability rules; Chainguard's PHP embargo dispute ([Socket](https://socket.dev/blog/php-embargo-dispute)) | Act on public advisories only. Never publish or ingest undisclosed vulns |
| **7. Maintenance treadmill** | NVD enrichment collapse; model IDs change constantly | One ecosystem. Reuse OSV and cve.now instead of scraping. Keep model aliases in one config file |
| **8. Cost runaway** | Uber's AI spend overshoot; AIxCC at ~$152 per task | Per-fix token and dollar caps, a maximum of 3 repair iterations, a daily ceiling, a kill switch |

On licensing: Renovate and Buttercup are AGPL, which matters if you ever host a service. PoCs from GitHub are a known malware vector, so never run them outside a sandbox with no network egress. Mark every PR as AI-generated and ship a license that disclaims warranty.

### Verification ladder (any failure stops the run and reports)

| Gate | Check |
|---|---|
| **0. Input** | Only structured sources (OSV, GHSA, KEV, EPSS) decide *whether* to act. News only creates watch signals |
| **1. Scope** | The fix class limits which files can change. Bumps touch only the manifest and lockfile. Repairs can add source files, with a capped diff and no workflow, CI, config, secrets or new dependencies |
| **2. Supply chain** | The target version's age vs the cooldown, provenance present if earlier versions had it, maintainer or publisher changes, new install scripts |
| **3. Build + full tests** | Run in an ephemeral sandbox with egress only to the registry. Tests must have been green on the base commit; otherwise the result is "not verifiable" and goes to a human |
| **4. Re-scan** | The target advisory is gone, and no new advisory of equal or higher severity has appeared |
| **5. Vuln-specific oracle** | Upstream regression test, a nuclei template, or a model-written failing-then-passing test (labeled as weaker) |
| **6. Cross-family review** | A different model family hunts for over-fixing, removed behavior and injected text. Advisory only |
| **7. Human approval** | Required for everything except Tier A |

**Merge tiers.** *Tier A* (auto-merge allowed, off by default): a patch or minor bump of a direct dependency with no source change, gates 0–4 green, cooldown satisfied or a clean fast-track, and decent test coverage. *Tier B* (one human approval): transitive overrides, major bumps, any LLM repair. *Tier C* (human plus a second reviewer, or don't automate at all): first-party code changes, auth and crypto paths, backports. Track these from day one: precision of "affected" calls, PRs opened vs merged, revert rate, time from CVE to PR, and dollars per PR.

**What makes it stand out on GitHub:** a tagline along the lines of "decides *when* to patch, not just how." A failure gallery and a strict-oracle eval table that cites PatchBench's 1.8x inflation lesson. SECURITY.md written first, plus an open "try to hack my patch bot" issue. Replay demos: holding a poisoned release, fast-tracking a reachable KEV item, and showing "no PR, here's why" next to Dependabot's spam. A per-PR cost footer. Avoid "autonomous," "zero-touch" and "AI hacker," and stay away from anything that sounds like ARTEX.

## How this was verified, and what got corrected

The research ran in **7 tracks**: open-source repos, commercial products, big-tech internal practice, academic work and AIxCC, community sentiment, the Chinese ecosystem, and architecture building blocks. A verdict-and-plan synthesis came after them. **Four independent checkers** then audited the tracks. A Haiku repo check queried **185 repos** against the GitHub API and confirmed **179**. Every stated star count was within 0.5%, and every license matched. The six misses were renames, archives, an incident label and a wrong owner. A Sonnet research and tech check covered 20 claims: **13 confirmed, 5 partly confirmed with corrections, 2 unverifiable, none contradicted**. A Sonnet market check covered **25 claims and found no fabrication in the main claims**. A separate fact-check covered a report you pasted from elsewhere. It confirmed the ARTEX core story and the Anthropic OAuth restrictions, and caught outdated billing details plus several loose figures.

**The big limitation:** this environment's network policy blocked many primary sites, including arXiv, NVD, DARPA, github.blog, Spotify and Shopify engineering, Reddit, Hacker News, X and most Chinese forums (Zhihu, FreeBuf, Kanxue). Many facts therefore rest on search-result extracts that quote the primary page, not on a full read. Reddit, X and HN comment-level sentiment, in particular, could not be observed directly.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. "Can AI patch CVEs?" is mostly answered: yes for version bumps, sometimes for breaking-change repair, and unreliably for free-form code fixes without a PoC. The open question is **"when should I patch, and how do I know the patch is safe?"** Attackers using patch-diffing agents push toward patching instantly. Worms hiding in fresh releases push toward waiting. Your vendors' bots answer neither, and they bury you in PRs. A small, honest tool that settles that conflict for one ecosystem, shows its evidence, and publishes its failures would be useful to you and credible to others. It would also make a great portfolio piece. The same tool as a company would be fighting GitHub on its home turf.

Spend your effort on verification, not on more autonomy. Every serious actor in this space, from Google and Meta to Snyk and Anthropic, kept the human merge gate for AI-written security fixes, and the best independent benchmark shows why. If your project can prove, with numbers, a narrower claim than everyone else's ("this bump is safe to merge now, and here's the evidence"), that will set it apart more than any model choice. Cheap models, Claude and Hermes are just plumbing. The policy layer and the oracle are the part worth building.
